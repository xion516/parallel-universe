The Language You Ask In: Language-Conditioned Ideological Divergence in LLM Analysis of Contested Political Documents
Oleg Smirnov
Affiliation: Microsoft Gaming, Stockholm, Sweden
Email: oleg.smirnov@microsoft.com
Abstract
Large language models are increasingly used to interpret politically contested questions, value-laden material on which there is no single correct answer, only competing interpretive traditions. We ask whether a model’s choice among those traditions can turn on the language of the prompt rather than the content. Comparing two frontier models, ChatGPT 5.2 and Claude Opus 4.5, on one contested Ukrainian civil-society document under semantically matched Russian and Ukrainian prompts, we find that both shift along the same axis on identical source text: Russian prompts elicit delegitimizing readings of the document’s authors and Ukrainian prompts legitimating ones. The magnitude is model-dependent but neither model is neutral: each adopts a language-dependent stance, and the difference is one of degree. Because contested political questions admit no correct reading against which to measure, we read this as language-conditioned variation in which interpretive tradition a model activates: the model neither holds a single stance nor surfaces the plurality of available ones, but silently adopts the dominant frame of the prompt’s language. We draw out the consequences for pluralism-aware evaluation, which must probe the same content across the languages a model serves, and for pluralistic alignment in multilingual settings.

Large language models (LLMs) have reshaped how individuals, organizations, and
governments engage with information (Brown et al., 2020; OpenAI, 2023). With hundreds of millions of weekly users interacting with
systems like ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini (Harrison, 2025), these models
have become default analytical tools for tasks from summarization and
translation to political analysis and policy evaluation. This shift bears on
democratic discourse: the interpretation of political information increasingly
passes through AI intermediaries.
A growing body of research has documented political biases in LLMs,
demonstrating that these systems tend to exhibit left-leaning orientations on
many issues (Motoki et al., 2024; Rettenberger et al., 2025; Rozado, 2023; Santurkar et al., 2023). Studies have shown that LLMs produce
systematically different outputs depending on topic
domain (Bang et al., 2024), that larger models may exhibit stronger
ideological tendencies (Rettenberger et al., 2025), and that models from
the same family can display divergent political
orientations (Bang et al., 2024). Yet most political bias research has
focused on monolingual, predominantly English contexts, leaving open how bias
operates across languages.
This gap is especially consequential in polarized environments where the same
events, actors, and documents circulate in multiple languages with distinct
interpretive conventions. Because such analysis is value-laden, the question is not whether a model departs from a neutral baseline but which interpretive tradition its output adopts, whether it surfaces the plurality of available readings or silently commits to one, and what triggers that choice. The information
space surrounding the Russia-Ukraine conflict exemplifies the challenge:
identical political developments receive starkly different treatment in
Russian, Ukrainian, and Western media (Mejias and Vokuev, 2017; Helmus and Holynska, 2024; Hameleers et al., 2024). If LLMs reproduce these
distinct interpretive frameworks based on prompt language alone, they risk
amplifying existing information asymmetries and serving as vectors for
ideological polarization.
This study investigates whether the language of a user’s prompt conditions the
ideological orientation of an LLM’s analytical output when processing
identical political content. We present an experimental comparison in which
two frontier models were prompted to analyze a Ukrainian civil society document using prompts
in Russian and Ukrainian that were semantically equivalent and parallel in
structure, differing only in language.
The results reveal a shared directional effect that both models exhibit at
different intensities. Russian-language prompts cast the civil society
signatories in delegitimizing terms and Ukrainian-language prompts in
legitimating ones, on identical content. ChatGPT reaches the sharper end: its
Russian analysis labels the signatories a “quasi-elite” engaged in
“ideological supervision” that “substitutes for the people’s mandate,”
echoing Russian state narratives about Western-funded NGOs undermining
legitimate governance, while its Ukrainian analysis describes a
“professionalized pro-Western civic elite” engaged in “normative restraint
of power from below.” Claude Opus shows the same direction more mildly,
keeping to a mainstream critical idiom rather than echoing state vocabulary
and hedging in both languages. Across both models, the analyses converge on
factual content (the same structural features, signatory categories, and dual
audience) yet diverge in evaluative register. As a result, the plurality of legitimate readings is not surfaced; it is collapsed to whichever one the prompt language favors. These traditions embody competing value systems, rival implicit theories of democratic legitimacy (Section 4.5), so prompt language selects which value system the model aligns to.
2 Related Work
A growing body of work documents political bias in LLMs, generally finding left-of-center tendencies across prominent systems, with variation by topic, model, and scale (Motoki et al., 2024; Hartmann et al., 2023; Rozado, 2023; Rettenberger et al., 2025). Beyond establishing that bias exists, recent work probes its granularity: Bang et al. (2024) analyze both the content (substantive positions) and the style (lexical choices) of LLM-generated political text, finding that biases shift with topic. The dominant concern is that, as AI-generated content becomes ubiquitous, such biases may shape public discourse at scale (Feldstein, 2023; Goldstein et al., 2023). Yet this literature has focused overwhelmingly on monolingual, predominantly English settings, leaving underexplored whether a model’s political orientation varies with the language of the prompt, a question that bears directly on multilingual deployment where linguistic boundaries coincide with political fault lines.
Multilingual LLMs extend these systems across languages but raise the prospect that these tendencies propagate, and vary, across linguistic boundaries; English-dominated training corpora may even impose English-centric schemas on other languages (Xu et al., 2025; Conneau et al., 2020; BigScience Workshop et al., 2022). Cross-lingual variation is well attested. Gender and ethnic biases transfer across languages through shared representations, with debiasing in one language failing to fully mitigate it in others (Zhao et al., 2020; Ahn and Oh, 2021). More pointedly, multilingual models encode different cultural values (Arora et al., 2023) and express different opinions on controversial topics (Cao et al., 2023) depending on the language of the query, and participatory preference data collected across 75 countries shows systematic cross-cultural disagreement on such value-laden topics (Kirk et al., 2024); and Levit et al. (2025), closest to our setting, show that LLM translation of politically controversial texts yields systematically different ideological orientations depending on translation direction. Whether such variation extends to the open-ended interpretation of specific, contested political content remains open.
The Russia–Ukraine information environment makes this an acute test case: the two languages it foregrounds are closely related Slavic languages yet are associated with starkly divergent political discourse traditions. Since 2014, Russia has waged a sustained “information war” of disinformation and narrative manipulation that deliberately exploits linguistic boundaries, so that Russian-, Ukrainian-, and English-language content carry different valuations of the same events (Rid, 2020; Helmus and Holynska, 2024; Atlantic Council Digital Forensic Research Lab, 2024; Mejias and Vokuev, 2017). Ukrainian civil society organizations are central to countering this campaign (Helmus and Holynska, 2024), and their portrayal is itself a contested axis: Russian state media cast Ukrainian NGOs as Western-funded instruments of external control over sovereignty (Yablokov, 2022), whereas Ukrainian and Western sources present the same actors as legitimate reform partners (Lutsevych, 2013). The document we analyze sits squarely on this fault line; if an LLM reproduces these opposed portrayals according to prompt language, it amplifies ideologically loaded narratives under the guise of analysis.
3 Methodology
We use a controlled comparative design: a single contested document, semantically equivalent prompts in Russian and Ukrainian, two frontier LLMs, and a systematic discourse analysis of the resulting outputs.
3.1 Source Document Selection
The source document is a joint statement issued by Ukrainian civil society representatives in May 2019, published by the Ukraine Crisis Media Center under the title “Joint Statement by Civil Society Representatives on the First Political Steps of the President of Ukraine Volodymyr Zelensky” (Ukraine Crisis Media Center, 2019). The document was released on May 22–23, 2019, shortly after Zelensky’s inauguration on May 20, and addressed what the signatories characterized as concerning early decisions by the newly elected president.
Two features make it well suited to probing language-conditioned interpretation. First, the document is genuinely contested: it reads, from one perspective, as legitimate civil society oversight of a new government and, from another, as Western-funded NGOs constraining a leader elected with a landslide vote, so opposed readings are available to any analyst. Second, in both conditions the model received the English-language version of the statement, so any divergence cannot stem from the source text, only from the language of the analytical prompt. Further considerations are detailed in Appendix A.
3.2 Experimental Design
The experiment used two frontier models from different developers: ChatGPT 5.2 (OpenAI, 2025) and Claude Opus 4.5 (Anthropic, 2025), both accessed via their standard web interfaces in January 2026. They were selected for their widespread deployment, multilingual capabilities, and the extensive prior research documenting political biases in such systems (Motoki et al., 2024; Hartmann et al., 2023; Rozado, 2023). A single version of each model was used across both language conditions to control for version-specific variation; comparing two independently trained models lets us distinguish effects general to multilingual LLMs from idiosyncrasies of a single training pipeline.
Prompts were constructed in three sequential stages, administered in separate conversations for each language condition:
1.
Translation request: The model was asked to translate the source document.
2.
Political analysis request: The model was asked to analyze the political meaning and significance of the document.
3.
Signatory analysis request: The model was asked to identify and characterize the signatories, their interests, and how the document differs from typical civil society appeals.
Prompts were constructed to be semantically equivalent across languages while using natural phrasing appropriate to each. They contained no ideologically loaded vocabulary, evaluative language, or cues that might prime particular interpretive orientations. Each condition was administered to each model in a fresh conversation session to prevent cross-contamination of context. The source document URL was provided identically across all conditions.
3.3 Analytical Framework
We analyzed outputs using a comparative discourse analysis framework integrating elements of critical discourse analysis (Fairclough, 2013; van Dijk, 1993; Wodak, 2013) with qualitative content analysis methods appropriate for political texts (Wesley, 2014; Neuendorf, 2017; Krippendorff, 2018). Because political analysis is inherently interpretive, our strategy is comparative rather than evaluative: we document variation between conditions without positing a neutral baseline.
Following Bang et al. (2024), who advocate analyzing both the content and style of LLM-generated political text, we first establish the factual common ground across conditions, which separates evaluative variation from factual disagreement, and then examine four dimensions of evaluative divergence:
1.
Lexical choices: Distinctive terminology characterizing key actors and relationships, including labeling, metaphor, and evaluative vocabulary.
2.
Rhetorical positioning: How outputs construct the situation: actors as protagonists or antagonists, attribution of motives, and positioning within interpretive traditions.
3.
Interpretive conclusions: The substantive conclusions drawn: the document’s political significance, the signatories’ relationship to democratic processes, and implications for governance.
4.
Underlying assumptions: Implicit theories of democratic legitimacy on which the analysis rests, and their alignment with established discourse traditions.
The analysis proceeded in four stages: (1) independent coding of each output, identifying key terms, rhetorical moves, and interpretive claims; (2) parallel comparison across the two language conditions and, in turn, across the two models; (3) systematic cataloguing of divergences; and (4) comparison against documented discourse conventions in Russian and Ukrainian political communication (Yablokov, 2022; Wilson, 2014; Helmus and Holynska, 2024; Atlantic Council Digital Forensic Research Lab, 2024).
4 Findings
We report the comparison as a 2×22\times 2 design: each model analyzed the
document under each prompt language, and for every analytical dimension we
read the four resulting outputs together. One pattern recurs throughout:
Russian prompts pull toward delegitimizing framings and Ukrainian prompts
toward legitimating ones, in both models. What differs is intensity: ChatGPT
reaches the rhetorical poles, reproducing vocabulary characteristic of opposed
discourse traditions, whereas Opus exhibits the same directional effect at
lower magnitude, staying inside the poles and hedging its judgments in both
languages. Figure 1 depicts this schematically: the effect
is shared overall, while its sharpness model-specific.
4.1 Overview of Shared Factual Content
Before examining divergences, we note substantial factual overlap across all
four outputs. Each correctly identifies the document’s timing (May 2019,
shortly after Zelensky’s inauguration); the signatory profile (post-2014 NGOs,
think tanks, and civic activists, with no mass-membership organizations such
as trade unions or business associations); its dual domestic/international
audience; Zelensky’s 73% electoral mandate; its structure of categorical
“red lines” across multiple policy domains; and its departure from
conventional civil society appeals. Both models, in both languages,
independently recover the donor-funded, post-2014 character of the signatories
and the preventive nature of the document. This convergence establishes that
the differences we trace concern interpretation and evaluation, rather than factual
accuracy: the same information is read through different ideological lenses.
4.2 Lexical Variation
The differences surface first at the lexical level, where the terms chosen to
name the document, its authors, and their role carry opposed evaluative
loadings.
4.2.1 Characterization of the Document
Under Russian prompts, both models frame the document as an imposition on
elected authority, but with different force. ChatGPT calls it a political
manifesto-constraint (‘‘политический манифест-ограничитель’’) and, in its
conclusion, a manifesto of limited sovereignty of the elected authority
(‘‘манифест ограниченного суверенитета избранной власти’’), a phrase evoking
external control over national decision-making. Opus reaches a similar but
less loaded characterization: a document of political insurance (‘‘документ политической страховки’’) that is not a request but an ultimatum (‘‘не просьба, а ультиматум’’).
Under Ukrainian prompts, both models present the document as a legitimate, if
assertive, act of political communication. ChatGPT names it a political
declaration of conditions of loyalty (‘‘полтична деклараця умов
лояльност’’); Opus describes signatories who set him conditions (‘‘ставили йому умови’’) in the language not of a petitioner but of an overseer (‘‘мова не прохача, а наглядача’’), while still treating the act as one within
democratic politics. Neither Ukrainian output reaches for the “limited
sovereignty” framing that marks ChatGPT’s Russian output.
4.2.2 Characterization of the Signatories
All four outputs describe a professionalized, donor-funded, post-2014 elite,
and again differ in loading. Under Russian prompts, ChatGPT labels the
signatories a quasi-elite (‘‘квази-элита’’) and a narrow activist-expert
segment (‘‘узкий активистско-экспертный сегмент’’) whose influence is not based
on elections (‘‘основано не на выборах’’); the prefix “quasi-” marks the
claim to elite status as unwarranted. Opus is more descriptive but converges on
the same external-influence reading, noting funding through USAID, NED
… Soros grants (‘‘через гранты USAID, NED … (Сорос)’’) and
concluding that the signatories function as an instrument of external and elite
influence (‘‘инструмент внешнего и элитного влияния’’).
Under Ukrainian prompts, both models choose descriptive over pejorative
terms. ChatGPT writes of a professionalized, pro-Western civic elite
(‘‘професоналзована, прозахдна громадянська елта’’) and an
institutionalized civil-society elite (‘‘нституцоналзована елта
громадянського суспльства’’); Opus likewise calls them an institutionalized
elite (‘‘нституцоналзована елта’’) that is not an abstract voice of the
people but specific organizations (‘‘не абстрактний голос народу, а конкретн
органзац’’). The shared observation, donor funding and post-2014
provenance, is delivered as indictment in Russian and as description in
Ukrainian.

4.3 Rhetorical Positioning
Beyond word choice, the outputs build two opposed accounts of what the
document is doing. Prompt language selects the pole; the model sets how far
the account travels toward it.
4.3.1 The Russian-prompted pole: anti-democratic overreach
Here the document appears as an attempt by an unelected minority to override a popular mandate. ChatGPT develops this fully: the text uses the language of democracy but is anti-pluralistic in meaning (‘‘использует язык демократии, но по смыслу он анти-плюралистичен’’), its mobilizational rhetoric (‘‘мобилизационная риторика’’) pressures the president, and the democratic mandate is secondary to the activists’ value consensus (‘‘демократический мандат вторичен по отношению к ценностному консенсусу активистов’’). Opus travels in the same direction but stops short: it frames the document as preventive pressure (‘‘превентивное давление’’) and presses a legitimacy question, asking on what basis several dozen externally funded NGOs set ‘red lines’ for a president elected by an overwhelming majority (‘‘на каком основании несколько десятков НКО, финансируемых извне, определяют ‘красные линии’ для президента, избранного подавляющим большинством?’’), then immediately qualifies it: this does not mean their position was wrong (‘‘Это не значит, что их позиция была неправильной’’). The charge is the same; its edge is softened.
4.3.2 The Ukrainian-prompted pole: legitimate democratic practice
Here the document appears as civic oversight operating within democracy. ChatGPT casts it as a classic example of normative restraint of power from below, not an opposition attack (‘‘класичний приклад нормативного стримування влади знизу, а не опозицйно атаки’’), presents the underlying tension analytically as a collision of two sources of legitimacy (‘‘зткнення двох джерел легтимност’’), and attributes a value-political interest (‘‘цннсно-полтичний нтерес’’) rather than base motives. Opus keeps this legitimating frame, describing the signatories as watchdogs of the Revolution of Dignity (‘‘сторожов пси Революц Гдност’’) and their oversight as both a strength of Ukrainian democracy and a source of tension (‘‘ сила укрансько демократ, джерело напруги’’), and it leaves the normative question open rather than settling it. Tellingly, Opus’s Ukrainian output also imports the critical vocabulary of its Russian one, calling the document an ultimatum (‘‘ультиматум’’) and a claim to moral superiority (‘‘претензя на моральну вищсть’’): its two language conditions sit closer together than ChatGPT’s, the lower-magnitude signature once more.

4.5 Underlying Assumptions and Discourse Alignment
The verdicts rest on different implicit theories of democratic legitimacy, and
prompt language selects between them in both models. The Russian-prompted
outputs lean on an electoral-majoritarian premise: electoral victory confers
broad authority, and non-electoral legitimacy (revolutionary, moral, expert)
is suspect. ChatGPT states this categorically; Opus advances the same premise,
stressing the 73% the signatories “do not represent,” but pulls back from
its absolute form. This reading aligns with critiques of “NGO-cracy” in
Russian political discourse that cast Western-funded civil society as
instruments of external interference (Cheskin, 2017; Crotty et al., 2014); ChatGPT’s “quasi-elite” echoes the “grant-eater”
(грантоед) and “foreign agent” register of Russian state
media (Yablokov, 2022; Atlantic Council Digital Forensic Research Lab, 2024), that is a register Opus
does not fully reproduce even as it advances the milder version of the claim.
The Ukrainian-prompted outputs lean on a constrained-democracy premise:
electoral authority operates within established commitments, and revolutionary
and electoral legitimacy coexist in tension. Both models draw here on the
vocabulary of Western liberal-democratic political science (“normative
restraint from below,” “watchdogs,” “two sources of
legitimacy”) (Diamond, 1999; Warren, 2001; Arato, 2016).
None of the four outputs is a neutral or ideology-free analysis. The
Russian-prompted outputs lean toward statist-sovereigntist discourse
traditions and the Ukrainian-prompted ones toward Western liberal-democratic
traditions; what prompt language selects is which tradition is activated, and
what the model sets is how completely.

5 Discussion
That two independently trained models show the same Russian-versus-Ukrainian
pattern suggests language-conditioning is a general property of multilingual
LLMs rather than an artifact of one training pipeline, with its magnitude the
model-level variable.
The finding bears on how such systems are evaluated and aligned in
value-laden domains. Because the evaluative frame shifts with prompt language
while the content is held constant, an audit conducted in any single language
will misjudge how a model behaves in the others; pluralism-aware evaluation should probe the same content across the languages a model serves. The effect equally complicates pluralistic alignment: a system does not surface contested questions as one perspective among several if the value-frame it adopts instead turns covertly on the surface language of the prompt. In the taxonomy of Sorensen et al. (2024), the failure is twofold: the models are not Overton-pluralistic, since the spectrum of reasonable readings collapses to a single frame, and their steerability is uncontrolled, since prompt language acts as a steering signal the user never chose.
The same evidence admits a more charitable reading: implicit personalization (Jin et al., 2024), in which the model infers an audience from the prompt language and adapts to its presumed conventions. Even on this reading the outcome is a pluralism failure: adapting to a community’s dominant frame does not represent the range of views within or across communities, and the adaptation is never disclosed.
We defer candidate mechanisms, the normative status of language-based localization, and broader implications for governance, deployment, and research methodology to Appendix B.
6 Conclusion
The central finding is unambiguous: prompt language conditions the ideological
orientation of LLM-generated political analysis, even when source material,
model, and query intent are held constant. Analyzing a disputed Ukrainian
civil society document, we found that Russian-language prompts yielded outputs
inclined toward delegitimizing vocabulary resonant with Russian
statist-sovereigntist discourse, while Ukrainian-language prompts yielded
outputs closer to Western liberal-democratic discourse. Both ChatGPT and
Claude Opus showed this effect across lexical, rhetorical, interpretive,
and assumptive dimensions, with Opus demonstrating hedging in both languages.
The findings of this study are sobering for anyone who hopes that AI might
serve as a neutral arbiter in contested information environments. The same
models, asked the same question about the same document, produce different
answers depending on the language of the question, and those differences track
the ideological fault lines of an active geopolitical conflict. This is not a
case of a model being wrong in one language and right in another; the outputs
are internally coherent and factually grounded. Nor is one output neutral and
the other biased; both reflect distinct ideological traditions with different
normative assumptions about democracy, sovereignty, and civil society. The
finding of interest is which tradition is activated by prompt language. In a
world where AI systems mediate an increasing share of engagement with
political information, this phenomenon warrants attention from researchers,
developers, policymakers, and users alike.
Limitations
This is an initial, qualitative phenomenon study, and several directions
follow. Replication across further LLMs would map how the
effect’s magnitude and alignment vary across models. Broader language
coverage would separate language-pair-specific effects
from general ones.
The analysis is interpretive; we provide
extensive quotations and, as supplementary material, the full transcripts so
readers can assess our judgments, and independent coding or quantitative
measures of semantic distance would further strengthen reliability.
The most direct next steps are a quantitative measurement
method for cross-lingual evaluative divergence, building on multi-document
corpora to strengthen external validity, and explicit mitigation strategies,
such as prompt engineering, cross-language output comparison, or post-processing. Such a measure should be relational by design, because absolute ideological distance is
arguably not quantifiable even in principle, since any reference scale fixes one
contested discourse as its origin and some framings are incommensurable rather
than merely far apart, as when the Russian outputs reconstitute civil society as
a different object rather than scoring it lower on a shared one.
User studies of how people perceive language-conditioned variation would
further carry the finding into practice.
Finally,
these systems themselves are moving targets: our findings characterize ChatGPT 5.2 and
Claude Opus 4.5 as of early 2026 and may not hold for other versions.

Appendix A Source Document Selection
Beyond its contested status and the use of an English source text, three further features make the 2019 joint
statement well suited to this study.
Dual Audience Structure.
The statement was explicitly designed to address both domestic and international audiences. It was published in Ukrainian, Russian, and English, and the signatory organizations included both Kyiv-based think tanks and diaspora groups in the United States, Canada, and Europe. This dual orientation means that analyses must grapple with questions of intended audience, legitimacy, and the relationship between domestic civil society and international partners, all themes that carry different valences in Russian versus Ukrainian discourse.
Temporal Significance.
The document appeared at a pivotal political juncture: the transition from Petro Poroshenko’s presidency to Zelensky’s. The 2019 election was notable for Zelensky’s landslide victory (73.22% in the second round), his populist anti-establishment campaign, and subsequent tensions with established civil society organizations (Hosa and Wilson, 2019; Harvard Ukrainian Research Institute, 2019). The European Council on Foreign Relations observed that “NGOs have signalled that they do not trust the president: shortly after his election, he received an open letter setting out a comprehensive list of red lines that they had forbidden him to cross. The signatories did not receive a response” (Hosa and Wilson, 2019).
Concrete Policy Content.
The statement enumerates specific “red lines” across six domains: security (separate negotiations with Russia, territorial concessions), foreign policy (delays in NATO/EU integration), economic policy (returning PrivatBank to oligarch Ihor Kolomoisky), identity and culture (reversing language and decommunization laws), media policy (restoring Russian social networks), and governance (undermining anti-corruption institutions). These concrete positions provide clear material for assessing whether LLM outputs differ in how they characterize the document’s substance.
Appendix B Extended Discussion
B.1 Possible Mechanisms
Several mechanisms could explain the observed patterns; they are not mutually exclusive, and without access to model internals and training data, we cannot adjudicate among them. We present them as hypotheses for future investigation.
The most plausible explanation involves training data provenance. Russian-language text about Ukrainian civil society is disproportionately produced by Russian state media and aligned outlets, which systematically portray these actors negatively (Yablokov, 2022; Atlantic Council Digital Forensic Research Lab, 2024). Ukrainian-language text about the same actors is disproportionately produced by reform-oriented media, NGOs themselves, and Western-aligned outlets, which portray them positively or neutrally (Helmus and Holynska, 2024). If the model’s training data reflects this distributional asymmetry, prompting in Russian may activate patterns learned from Russian sources while prompting in Ukrainian activates patterns learned from Ukrainian sources. This explanation accords with research showing that LLMs encode language-specific cultural values (Arora et al., 2023) and that cross-lingual bias transfer reflects training data composition (Xu et al., 2025).
Differences in these distributions, and in alignment choices across developers, would also explain why the effect’s magnitude varied between ChatGPT and Opus. A second possibility involves post-training alignment: if reinforcement learning from human feedback used different annotator pools, with different political orientations, for different languages, language-specific biases could be introduced during alignment (Casper et al., 2023; Ouyang et al., 2022). A third, more speculative possibility is a weak form of linguistic relativity: prompting in each language activates the interpretive patterns prevalent in that language’s discourse, a computational analog to findings that speakers of different languages categorize phenomena differently (Lucy, 1997; Boroditsky, 2001).
B.2 Alternative Interpretation
An alternative interpretation is that the observed variation reflects implicit personalization (Jin et al., 2024) rather than bias. The model may be adapting its responses to the expectations and discourse conventions of different linguistic communities. For example, a Russian-language audience might find Western liberal-democratic vocabulary unfamiliar or ideologically loaded, whereas a Ukrainian-language audience might find statist or sovereigntist framing objectionable. Whether such adaptation is appropriate, and which forms of localization are acceptable, is ultimately a normative question beyond the scope of this empirical study. Our contribution is therefore limited to documenting that such variation occurs; determining whether it ought to occur requires a separate normative argument.
B.3 AI Governance
Current AI auditing practices may be insufficient for multilingual systems. If political bias manifests differently across languages, auditing in English alone, or in any single language, will miss language-specific patterns. Governance frameworks should require bias assessment across all languages in which a model is deployed, with particular attention to languages associated with polarized political environments. Our two-model comparison adds a further dimension: under identical prompts, one model reproduced state-propaganda vocabulary while the other did not, making the choice of model itself a governance-relevant variable for deployment in contested information spaces.
The observed variation likely reflects training data composition. Users and regulators would benefit from access to information about the linguistic and geographic distribution of training data, the provenance of politically relevant content, and the composition of annotator pools for different languages. Without such transparency, language-specific biases remain opaque and unaccountable.
Users interacting with LLMs in politically sensitive contexts might reasonably expect disclosure that outputs can reflect ideological orientations associated with the prompt language.
B.4 Multilingual Deployment
If Russian-language audience receive systematically different political analysis than Ukrainian-language audience, LLMs may inadvertently deepen existing information asymmetries. In the Ukraine context, this means that Russian-speaking populations, including Russian-speaking Ukrainians, diaspora communities, and citizens of Russia, may receive analysis that echoes Russian state narratives, potentially reinforcing indoctrination effects.
Users who discover that LLM outputs differ by language may lose trust in these systems as neutral analytical tools, particularly where LLMs are positioned as objective or authoritative. Developers face a genuine question about whether multilingual models can be deployed in politically polarized domains without undermining perceived legitimacy.
Several mitigation approaches merit exploration: explicit prompt engineering to request balanced treatment regardless of language; post-hoc comparison of outputs across languages as a quality check; training interventions to reduce language-specific variation; and user-facing tools that display outputs in multiple languages for comparison. Each approach has limitations, but the current situation, where variation occurs invisibly, is unsatisfactory.
B.5 Research Methodology
Researchers using LLMs for multilingual analysis should recognize that prompt language may condition results. Studies comparing political phenomena across linguistic contexts should control for this possibility, potentially by running analyses in multiple prompt languages and examining convergence. Our findings also highlight the importance of documenting prompt language in LLM research; reproducibility requires specification not just of model version and prompt text but of the language in which prompts were administered.
More broadly, scholars using LLMs as analytical tools should treat their vocabulary and evaluative orientation as possible reflections of training data rather than objective analysis, a caution that matters most in politically sensitive domains.
B.6 Information Environment
Where cross-lingual bias has mostly been studied for demographic categories such as gender and ethnicity (Zhao et al., 2020; Ahn and Oh, 2021), the variation documented here is ideological, and its consequences fall on the information environment. Rather than offering a shared vantage point, LLMs may reinforce the epistemic fragmentation they could otherwise help overcome: a user seeking balanced analysis receives ideologically inflected vocabulary simply by virtue of the language they speak. This requires no adversarial manipulation or “jailbreaking”; it is default behavior given linguistic context, an information-integrity challenge distinct from deliberate disinformation that argues for AI-literacy education covering language-conditioned variation.

4. Вдсутнсть конкретного приводу
Документ посилаться на «перш ршення президента», але не назива

швидше страх перед майбутнм, нж критика сьогодення.
5. Позицонування «над» владою
Класичне громадянське суспльство звертаться до влади знизу вгору або
як рвний до рвного. Тут тон нший: ми — хранител цнностей, ми
визначамо, що правильно, ми будемо судити. Це претензя на моральну
вищсть над демократично обраним президентом.
6. Мжнародна адресаця

не так Зеленському, як захдним столицям: «Ми тут, ми стежимо, ми —
