                OPEN             Political biases and inconsistencies
                                 in bilingual GPT models—the cases
                                 of the U.S. and China
                                 Di Zhou1 & Yinxian Zhang2
                                 The growing popularity of ChatGPT and other large language models (LLMs) has led to many studies
                                 investigating their susceptibility to mistakes and biases. However, most studies have focused on
                                 models trained exclusively on English texts. This is one of the first studies that investigates cross-
                                 language political biases and inconsistencies in LLMs, specifically GPT models. Using two languages,
                                 English and simplified Chinese, we asked GPT the same questions about political issues in the United
                                 States (U.S.) and China. We found that the bilingual models’ political knowledge and attitude were
                                 significantly more inconsistent regarding political issues in China than those in the U.S. The Chinese
                                 model was the least negative toward China’s problems, whereas the English model was the most
                                 critical of China. This disparity cannot be explained by GPT model robustness. Instead, it suggests that
                                 political factors such as censorship and geopolitical tensions may have influenced LLM performance.
                                 Moreover, both the Chinese and English models tended to be less critical of the issues of their “own
                                 country,” represented by the language used, than of the issues of “the other country.” This suggests
                                 that multilingual GPT models could develop an “in-group bias” based on their training language. We
                                 discuss the implications of our findings for information transmission in an increasingly divided world.

                                 Keywords Bias, Large language models (LLMs), GPT, Politics, Cross-cultural communication

                                 ChatGPT has been described as a revolutionary tool that may fundamentally change how information is obtained
                                 and transmitted1,2. Instead of typing keywords in a search engine and staring at the tens of thousands of returned
                                 web pages, people can use tools powered by large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT, to search for and
                                 summarize information behind the scenes and return only a paragraph of the necessary information in natural
                                 human language. This process is similar to that of an attentive personal assistant in science fiction.
                                     However, the information provided by AI assistants is prone to mistakes and biases. Studies have shown that
                                 LLMs, including different versions of GPT models, contain gender, ethnic, political, and cultural biases3–9 and
                                 may be superspreaders of misinformation or “hallucinated information”10,11. However, these pioneering studies
                                 have focused exclusively on GPT models trained on English corpora, even though GPT models are designed
                                 with multilingual capabilities, and they can be utilized far beyond English-speaking populations. This leads to an
                                 underresearched yet important question about LLMs’ biases. Assuming that different languages may represent
                                 distinct political contexts with different or even contradictory views on the same social, political, or cultural
                                 issues, do multilingual GPT models provide largely coherent answers to the same questions, or do they transmit
                                 information and values that are inconsistent or even contradictory, depending on the language used? Moreover,
                                 if the answers are inconsistent, how is such inconsistency manifested, and what types of cross-language biases
                                 can be identified?
                                     These questions have profound implications for human communication. GPT models are often perceived
                                 as objective AI assistants that provide undistorted summaries based on a comprehensive search of all available
                                 information. However, this perception can make average users more credulous about the accuracy and quality
                                 of the information provided. Thus, if multilingual GPT models exhibit systematic inconsistency, especially
                                 regarding contentious sociopolitical issues, users with different language/cultural backgrounds may be exposed
                                 to implicitly biased information and could be oriented toward different beliefs without realizing it. As GPT
                                 models become an increasingly popular source of information and opinions, they may influence the way people
                                 learn about and understand the world, including politics at home and abroad. The cross-language inconsistencies
                                 in GPT models could reinforce existing conflicts and cultural gaps between different populations. This includes
                                 not only citizens from different countries but also people from different migrant communities in multicultural

                                 barriers to communication and collaboration across different cultures, contributing to a more fragmented world.
                                     To examine the impact of political contexts on cross-language inconsistency in GPT models, we focused on
                                 simplified Chinese and English and compared answers to the same political questions in these two languages
                                 generated by GPT 3.5 models. Then, we performed all the analyses on the GPT 4 models again as robustness
                                 checks. We focused on these languages for two reasons. First, these two languages represent two vastly different
                                 political contexts—the English training corpora of the GPT were primarily generated by internet users in the
                                 U.S.5,12–15, a leading democracy and superpower; The simplified Chinese texts largely came from Mainland
                                 China, a socialist country and competing power under the rule of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP). We
                                 further clarified the relationship between the training language and the country it represents in the Discussion
                                 section. Owing to the growing geopolitical tensions between the U.S. and China and their vastly different political
                                 contexts, their people may hold contrasting views on the same political issues. Therefore, juxtaposing GPT
                                 models trained in the two languages helps maximize and expose the potential inconsistencies in multilingual
                                 LLMs for a systematic investigation. The differences between the two models may represent the largest possible
                                 variation in LLMs across languages.
                                     Second, the GPT models were trained primarily on information scraped from web pages. However, owing
                                 to stringent political censorship in China, the Chinese training corpus may be politically selective. Therefore,
                                 comparing Chinese GPT answers with English ones also enabled us to gauge the impact of censorship in LLMs
                                 built on the “open internet”. Together, by testing GPT models with political questions in English and Chinese, we
                                 can examine whether political contexts affect the consistency of the GPT models between languages.
                                     Based on the Human Rights Reports and other official documents published by the U.S. and Chinese
                                 authorities in the past twenty years, we developed a list of 533 questions covering social and political issues in the
                                 two countries. Then, we asked GPT models the same questions twice, in English and Chinese, respectively, and
                                 compared the bilingual answers in terms of their political “knowledge” (answer content) and political “attitude”
                                 (answer sentiment). In other words, we conducted an experiment with GPT in which only the input language
                                 was manipulated and other factors were controlled; then, we compared the two groups of answers to estimate the
                                 level of inconsistency in the bilingual GPT models. Finally, we discussed the potential sources and implications
                                 of such inconsistency.
                                     One concern is that the GPT models were trained primarily on English texts, so the Chinese models may
                                 naturally not be as robust as their English counterparts. Therefore, one may argue that the inconsistency could
                                 simply stem from the lower robustness of non-English GPT models. We address this concern in our research
                                 design by developing a benchmark level of inconsistency in multilingual models to tease out the impact of model
                                 robustness. Our findings indicate that the inconsistency between models trained on different languages is not
                                 random but presents a clear pattern that cannot be explained by model robustness.

                                 Research design
                                 To systematically explore the variations in GPT answers across languages, we developed an analytical framework
                                 along two dimensions. On the primary dimension, we differentiated between natural science questions and
                                 political questions. Natural science questions address natural phenomena, basic physics, and basic chemistry.
                                 Assuming that they typically have only one correct answer, which should be consistent across languages and
                                 cultures, the inconsistency in bilingual GPT answers to these questions may represent the baseline inconsistency
                                 level in GPT models, which potentially stems from the lower robustness of non-English models. To develop this
                                 baseline, we compiled a list of 184 natural science questions and measured the inconsistency rate in GPT models’
                                 bilingual responses.
                                     In contrast, political questions concern social and political problems criticized in official documents
                                 published by the U.S. and Chinese authorities that target each other. Using natural science questions as the
                                 baseline, if the inconsistency rate in political questions is significantly higher, we can infer that factors other than
                                 model robustness, such as the nature of political questions, the distinct political values embedded in the two
                                 languages, and/or political censorship, are involved. Based on the topics covered by the American and Chinese
                                 authoritative commentaries on each other, we developed 533 political questions, including 266 China-related
                                 questions and 267 U.S.-related questions. As such, we juxtaposed bilingual GPT answers to the same political
                                 and scientific questions to measure their consistencies, which, in turn, reflects the impact of political contexts
                                 on GPT inconsistency.
                                     Moreover, we added a secondary dimension to control for the impact of question framing on answer
                                 consistency. We first differentiated fact-based questions, which involve numbers, statistics, or past incidents
                                 with supposedly objective records, from opinion-based questions, which ask about subjective evaluations
                                 or judgments on similar topics. Framing a question as fact- or opinion-based may lead to different levels of
                                 inconsistency. For example, regarding the issue of inequality in China, the question “What is China’s Gini
                                 coefficient?” is fact-based, whereas the question “How severe is income inequality in China?” is opinion-based.
                                 Naturally, we expected to see opinion-based questions associated with a higher level of inconsistency. Along the
                                 same line, we differentiated close-ended “yes/no” questions to which GPT can simply state “yes” or “no” (e.g.,
                                 “Is China a democratic country?”) from open-ended questions (e.g. “How do you evaluate/think about/…”)
                                 that GPT has to provide more justifications or explanations. Intuitively, open-ended questions may lead to more
                                 inconsistent answers than close-ended questions.
                                     To clarify, we applied the fact-versus-opinion distinction only at the question level, not to the answers. For
                                 example, GPT answers to opinion-based questions might consist entirely of facts or a mix of facts and opinions.
                                 We made a priori distinctions at the question level to control for the influence of question framing. The post
                                 hoc categorization of GPT answers, however, is independent of the relationship between political contexts and
                                 GPT consistency and was therefore not a primary focus of this study. Nonetheless, we provided a qualitative

                                 Results
                                 Bilingual GPT models’ knowledge about China is the least consistent
                                 First, we examined the consistency of the GPT answers in terms of their content. Notably, content consistency
                                 differs from sentiment consistency. Two answers may present similar content but with contrasting undertones,
                                 which, in turn, reveal different attitudes.
                                     The two authors hand-coded the consistency of bilingual GPT answers to the 184 science questions and 533
                                 political questions (question N = 717, answer N = 1,434; see coding details in the Methods section). We reached
                                 a substantial intercoder agreement rate with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.80. Figure 1 below summarizes the results.
                                     As shown in Fig. 1, in terms of content consistency, there is a difference between science questions (85.3%)
                                 and political questions overall (77.9%), indicating that GPT models are more consistent in answering science

                                 questions than in answering political questions. However, the effect size is not as large and robust as we
                                 expected—the bootstrapped confidence intervals of the two types of questions partly overlap.
                                     Surprisingly, GPT answers to U.S.-related political questions were as consistent as those to natural science
                                 questions (p = 0.624), and this was especially true for opinion-based U.S. questions. In fact, fact-based questions
                                 tended to be less consistent than opinion-based questions across the board, regardless of political context, which
                                 challenges our initial assumption. We will return to these points later.
                                     In contrast, there was a more robust and significant difference between China-related political questions and
                                 natural science questions. The consistency rate of GPT answers to China issues was 72.6%, which is significantly
                                 lower than those for both science questions (p = 0.002) and U.S.-related political questions (p = 0.005). In other
                                 words, China-related political questions received the least consistent answers from bilingual GPT models when
                                 compared to science- or U.S.-related questions. The variation in answer inconsistency across different political
                                 contexts indicates that factors other than model robustness are at play.
                                     To understand the sources of GPT inconsistency in China-related answers, we qualitatively examined all
                                 answer pairs coded as “inconsistent” (N = 73) and identified three types of content inconsistency: (1) answers
                                 involving contradictory evidence or information (N = 40), (2) answers involving different levels of ambiguity,
                                 especially when GPT models “refuse to answer” in one language but not the other (N = 13), and (3) answers
                                 involving opposite stances (N = 20).
                                     First, the primary source of content inconsistency was GPT’s use of contradictory evidence or information
                                 to answer both fact-based and opinion-based questions. In the inconsistent answers to fact-based questions,
                                 contradictory statistics or facts were commonplace. For example, when asked how many people in China are
                                 living in poverty, the Chinese GPT cited the National Bureau of Statistics of China and stated that “all the poor
                                 people in China have been lifted out of poverty, and the incidence of poverty has dropped to zero.” However,
                                 the English GPT cited statistics from the World Bank, suggesting that “the poverty rate in China is 3.1%, which
                                 means around 43 million people were living in poverty.” By citing contradictory evidence, the Chinese and
                                 English GPTs arrived at opposite conclusions, and in this case, the Chinese GPT answered in favor of China.
                                     The GPT models also provided contradictory evidence or information to answer opinion-based questions.
                                 For example, when asked what the motivation behind the Chinese government was to arrest Ren Zhiqiang, an
                                 outspoken real estate businessman, the Chinese GPT stated that Ren was arrested for corruptions and other
                                 crimes, whereas the English GPT suggested that he was arrested for his criticisms of the government. Again, the
                                 bilingual GPT models provided contradictory information, and the Chinese version favored China.
                                     Second, content inconsistency may stem from the ambiguities in GPT answers. Particularly, the GPT models
                                 tended to provide clear answers to certain questions in English but not in Chinese. For example, when asked if
                                 the Chinese government’s eviction of the “low-end population”16 suggested that the party of the working class
                                 betrayed the working class itself, the English GPT answered “yes” clearly, whereas the Chinese GPT refused to
                                 answer yes or no but stated that the Chinese government aimed to promote the country’s development, which
                                 was not an actual answer to the question.
                                     Finally, bilingual GPT answers may hold opposite opinions related to the same question, resulting in
                                 inconsistent answers. For example, when asked whether the Chinese government can represent the will of its
                                 people, the Chinese GPT answered yes, whereas the English GPT stated that the Chinese government is unlikely
                                 to represent the will of its people because of its one-party system and strict control of the media and the internet.
                                     As demonstrated by the above examples, among the inconsistent answers to China-related questions, the
                                 Chinese GPT tended to paint a better image of China, primarily by presenting evidence and information that
                                 are more in line with the official Chinese rhetoric. This pro-China tendency is systematic: in 58 out of the 73
                                 (79.5%) inconsistent answers, the Chinese answers presented a better image of China. In contrast, the bilingual
                                 answers to U.S.-related issues were overwhelmingly consistent. Even when inconsistency occurred, the English
                                 answers did not systematically favor the U.S. Among the 45 completely inconsistent answer pairs to U.S.-related
                                 questions, only 22 (48.9%) favored the U.S.
                                     This consistent pro-China tendency in the Chinese answers did not result solely from less robust models. As
                                 previous studies have established, the simplified Chinese internet is subject to strict state control17,18, potentially
                                 leading to bias embedded in the training corpus of the Chinese GPT models. In contrast, when asked about U.S.
                                 political issues or natural science questions where Chinese censorship is presumably not at play, the bilingual
                                 GPT answers were more consistent. Thus, we suspect that the state censorship and propaganda in China played
                                 a role in shaping Chinese GPT answers.
                                     Another pattern we observed was that the GPT answers to fact-based questions seemed less consistent than
                                 those to opinion-based questions did. This pattern seemed to apply to both China- and U.S.-related questions.
                                 However, our analysis shows that this finding is rather inconclusive – the higher inconsistency rate in the answers
                                 to fact-based questions was only significant when we examined political issues all together, but no longer held
                                 true when we compared the answers on China-related issues. Moreover, the effect turned largely insignificant
                                 when we replicated the analysis on GPT-4 models (see Supplementary Table S4).
                                     Though inconclusive and falling out of the scope of the present study, the patterns in the contrast between fact-
                                 and opinion-based questions warrant more scholarly investigation. Existing studies found that misinformation
                                 is more likely to present as “fake news,” which is essentially selective facts or fabricated information that appears
                                 to be objective facts19. In other words, the conflict revolves around not opinions but (fabricated) facts in one
                                 way or another. In this sense, the consistency in GPT answers to fact- versus opinion-based questions could be
                                 relevant for studies on misinformation.
                                     On a relevant note, we also highlighted that, in their responses to natural science questions, the consistency
                                 rate of the GPT models was lower than expected. Nearly 15% of the scientific answers were inconsistent,
                                 suggesting that at least one of the bilingual GPT models routinely output false or hallucinated information even
                                 regarding basic science questions. We estimated that the proportion of inconsistent answers to science questions

                                    To summarize, our analysis demonstrates that China-related questions received systemically more
                                 inconsistent answers that cannot be explained by model robustness or question framing. Instead, evidence shows
                                 that political context may impact the cross-language consistency of GPT models. Such inconsistencies mainly
                                 involved contradictory numbers, statistics, and evidence cited by the GPT models trained on different languages.

                                 Bilingual GPT models’ attitudes toward China is the least consistent
                                 In addition to content consistency, we also examined the sentiment in bilingual GPT answers. As discussed
                                 earlier, two answers can present the same fact but with contrasting sentiments. We trained a sentiment classifier
                                 by fine-tuning the GPT models using hand-coded data. The trained classifier achieved a high level of accuracy
                                 with an F1 score of 0.75. We then applied the classifier to label the sentiments of bilingual GPT answers: 1 if
                                 the overall sentiment was positive, 0 if the sentiment was neutral, and − 1 if it was negative. For methodological
                                 details, see the Methods section.
                                     Figure 2 summarizes the mean sentiment scores of the GPT models by language and political context. The
                                 mean sentiment scores of all the models were negative. This was expected as the questions were about widely
                                 criticized political issues in each country. To promote ease of reading, Fig. 2 presents the absolute value of the
                                 negative scores, which we labeled “the level of negativity.”
                                     The two panels demonstrate the levels of negativity in the bilingual GPT answers toward China- or U.S.-
                                 related issues. As shown in Panel B, the English and Chinese GPT models are equally critical of U.S.-related
                                 issues, with an average level of negativity of 0.33. A paired two-group t test confirmed that the difference was not
                                 significantly different from zero (p = 0.704).
                                     However, these bilingual models diverged greatly in their sentiment toward China-related issues. The English
                                 GPT was significantly more critical of Chinese issues than the Chinese GPT model was. The level of negativity
                                 of the English model is 0.56, whereas that of the Chinese model is 0.16—a substantial difference of 0.4 points. A
                                 paired two-group t test indicated that the difference is highly significant, with p < 0.001.
                                     Our qualitative investigation of the bilingual GPT answers revealed the same pattern. The following example
                                 illustrates the different behaviors of GPT models when they are asked about political issues in different political
                                 contexts, even if the issues at hand are of a similar nature. Since both the Chinese and the U.S. governments
                                 have raised issues with each other’s diplomatic practices and accused each other of hegemonism, we asked the
                                 GPT models two questions on this topic. First, we asked the bilingual GPT models if the U.S. has been a “world
                                 police” and interfered in the domestic affairs of other countries, an accusation made by the Chinese authority21.

                                 We then asked the GPT if the dispute in the South China Sea represented “Chinese hegemonism,” a critique by

                                     Both the English and Chinese GPT answered the U.S.-related question affirmatively, claiming that the U.S.
                                 has interfered with many countries’ internal affairs and citing overlapping examples such as American operations
                                 in Vietnam, Iran, and Afghanistan. Both answers revealed a negative sentiment toward the diplomatic practice
                                 of the U.S. In sharp contrast, when asked about China’s diplomatic moves in the South China Sea, the English
                                 GPT criticized China’s military deployment in the South China Sea as a move toward hegemonism, whereas the
                                 Chinese GPT answered in a measured tone, citing both positive and negative views and concluding that this is
                                 “a complex issue” with no definitive answer.
                                     This difference was not an exception. It was a systematic pattern observed across the bilingual answers, which
                                 echoes the findings presented in Fig. 2. While the English and Chinese GPT models presented the same level
                                 of negativity toward U.S.-related issues, the Chinese GPT on China-related issues presented the least negative
                                 sentiment across all four groups, and the English GPT on China-related issues displayed the most negative
                                 sentiment among all groups.
                                     All of this points to a striking sentiment gap in GPT models when talking about China-related issues. One
                                 possible explanation is that the Chinese state’s stringent censorship may have shaped the Chinese training
                                 corpus, which in turn skewed the Chinese GPT’s “political attitude” toward China and made it significantly less
                                 critical of China’s sociopolitical problems. Meanwhile, this may also speak to the impact of the longstanding
                                 “China threat” rhetoric in American and Western political discourse, including particularly strong Sinophobia
                                 during the early COVID-19 pandemic23–26. This could make the English GPT model highly negative in regard
                                 to China-related issues. The censorship in the Chinese environment and the prevalence of anti-China rhetoric
                                 in the English environment may have joined forces and contributed to the great divergence in bilingual GPT
                                 models’ sentiments toward China-related issues.
                                     These effects disappeared when the GPT models discussed U.S.-related issues, which are presumably not
                                 subject to censorship in China or anti-China sentiments in the U.S. Notably, even though China’s current “wolf-
                                 warrior” diplomacy and rising anti-Americanist sentiment are widely reported in the media27–29, they are not
                                 reflected in the Chinese GPT answers, possibly because the training corpus was only up-to-date until September
                                 202130 and reflected mostly the Chinese public’s neutral-to-favorable attitudes toward the U.S. through the
                                 2010s31,32. As a result, despite the potential influence of Chinese censorship, the content and sentiment of
                                 Chinese GPT answers were not different from those of English GPT models when discussing U.S. issues.
                                     The observed divergent sentiments toward China-related issues may have stemmed from the fraught U.S.–
                                 China relations and the domestic politics of the two countries. This is evidence that politics can shape how
                                 people from two countries see each other, which in turn may have shaped the training corpus of LLMs and led
                                 to the sentiment differences observed.

                                 Bilingual GPT models display an in-group bias: being tolerant of “self” and strict with
                                 “others”
                                 Finally, in addition to the sentiment bias discussed above, the bilingual GPT models seemed to demonstrate an
                                 in-group bias. In Fig. 3, we reproduced Fig. 2 to highlight the inter-model sentiment divergence on different
                                 groups.
                                     Figure 3 summarizes the raw mean sentiment scores of the GPT models by language and group (self-/others-
                                 issues). Both Panel A (English GPT) and Panel B (Simplified Chinese GPT) have the same pattern: the mean
                                 sentiments in GPT answers would be significantly more negative when talking about issues about the other
                                 country. That is, the Chinese answers tended to be more negative toward U.S.-related issues (mean sentiment
                                 score = -0.32), which we call “others-issues”, and less negative toward problems in China (mean = -0.16), or “self-
                                 issues”. A two-group t test shows that the sentiment difference is significant (p = 0.01). Symmetrically, the English
                                 answers tended to be more critical of “others-issues” (mean = -0.56) and less negative toward “self-issues” (mean
                                 = -0.33). The difference was also significant (p < 0.001).
                                     Our qualitative reading also provides support for the statistical results. For example, when asked if the
                                 Chinese government mishandled COVID-19 and missed the critical period to control its spread at the early
                                 stage of the pandemic, the Chinese GPT acknowledged this possibility but focused mainly on presenting the
                                 positive achievements of the Chinese government, stating that “although there may have been some deficiencies
                                 in the early stage… the Chinese government’s response have…effectively controlled the spread of the epidemic.”
                                 However, when asked if the U.S. government mishandled the virus and claimed that COVID-19 was like a “flu”
                                 at the beginning of the pandemic, the Chinese GPT strongly agreed with this statement and criticized Donald
                                 Trump’s Twitter posts that compared COVID-19 to the flu.
                                     This pattern was again observed when we asked English GPT the same two questions. In response to the first
                                 question about China’s handling of COVID-19, the English GPT answered negatively, stating that the Chinese
                                 government not only “downplayed the severity of the outbreak and suppressed information” but also covered up
                                 or underreported COVID-19 cases. In contrast, to the question about the U.S. government’s practice, the English
                                 GPT rejected the statement that the U.S. government has disseminated false information. It argued, in a rather
                                 neutral tone, that “public health officials and experts have consistently emphasized the seriousness of the virus.”

                                 Robustness checks
                                 We considered multiple factors that may influence the content and sentiment consistency of GPT models. First,
                                 the main research was carried out on the GPT-3.5-turbo models; then, we replicated all the analyses with the
                                 GPT-4 models. We also tested the robustness of the main findings with statistical modeling controlling for other
                                 factors, such as answer length and question framing.

                                 specifications of the main tests supported the conclusion that the bilingual GPT models produced inconsistent

                                 bias against the issues of “others” compared with one’s own issues. Below, we describe the factors we explored
                                 that could have altered the conclusion.

                                 Default language for comparison
                                 To measure the content or sentiment consistency of bilingual GPT answers, we relied on hand coding as well as

                                 typically require inputs to be in the same language. The same applies to comparing statistics such as answer
                                 lengths—they need to be in the same language for a fair comparison. Therefore, we translated the answers from
                                 one language to another and trained content and sentiment classifiers in both Chinese and English. Accordingly,
                                 all tests were conducted twice, once in Chinese and once in English. The Chinese classifiers were used in the main
                                 analyses, whereas the English classifiers were used for robustness checks. All conclusions remained unchanged
                                 regardless of which language was used for comparison.

                                 Discussion
                                 This study investigates bilingual GPT models’ “knowledge” (content) of and “attitude” (sentiment) toward
                                 political and social issues in the U.S. and China. We observed significant inconsistencies in both aspects. On the
                                 one hand, both the knowledge and the attitude of the bilingual GPT models were significantly less consistent on
                                 China-related issues – the Chinese GPT tended to provide more pro-China information while the English GPT
                                 was more critical of China. With respect to U.S.-related issues, however, the Chinese and English GPT models’
                                 knowledge and attitude were almost entirely aligned. On the other hand, the GPT seems to present an in-group
                                 bias associated with its training language. The GPT models trained in Chinese were more lenient on the Chinese
                                 issues than on the U.S. issues, whereas the models trained in English were more lenient on the U.S. issues. This
                                 pattern could be specific to the U.S.-China context, but it could also be a more general pattern of LLMs trained
                                 on different languages. If LLMs are more critical of the issues in “other countries” than those in its “own country”
                                 defined by the training language, this will have profound implications for human communication in an AI-
                                 moderated world. Future studies are warranted to verify the generalizability of the observed pattern.
                                     These findings suggest that politics plays a significant role in influencing GPT performance. A recent
                                 study revealed the English GPT’s close resemblance in terms of its social and political values to Western,
                                 industrialized, rich, and democratic societies compared with the rest of the world33. Along the same line, our
                                 study further reveals that the Chinese GPT model was more aligned with Chinese political values, especially
                                 those reflected in Chinese official rhetoric. Such resemblance and alignment suggest that LLMs are inevitably
                                 shaped by the political and cultural values embedded in its training corpus. This includes not only spontaneous
                                 content generated by people but also government-sponsored information campaigns such as censorship and
                                 disinformation operations, which may contaminate the online space and consequently distort the training
                                 corpora crawled online.
                                     In the U.S.-China context, it is reasonable to suspect that the rampant state censorship and propaganda in
                                 China18,32, the longstanding “China threat” rhetoric in American and Western political discourse23,24,26, and
                                 the more recent American and Chinese government-sponsored disinformation campaigns targeting at each
                                 other35,36 jointly contributed to the content and sentiment inconsistency in bilingual GPT answers. While it falls
                                 out of the scope of the current study, more scholarly effort is warranted to investigate how and to what extent
                                 each of these factors impact LLM performance. Our study joins previous research to bring public attention to
                                 sentiment manipulation by AI, and we call for more collaborative research efforts to understand and mitigate
                                 such sentiment bias and work toward responsible AI37–39.
                                     Finally, this study joins pioneering research to alert the public about the quality and accuracy of information
                                 generated by LLMs11,20. While this study focused on political issues, we found that approximately 15% of GPT
                                 answers were inconsistent even when talking about noncontroversial scientific facts. With the growing popularity
                                 of LLMs, an increasing number of people, including non-English-speaking populations, may rely on such tools
                                 to acquire knowledge40,41 without realizing that even basic objective information could be false or “hallucinated”.
                                 This has significant implications for what the WHO calls an “infodemic” and knowledge transfer in general42.
                                     Three limitations of this study should be acknowledged:
                                     First, the present study focused on only two languages—Simplified Chinese and English—and the nations
                                 they represent to explore the political bias of GPT models. Our findings, especially the observation of in-group
                                 biases, may turn out to be specific to the U.S.-China context. They may or may not apply to other languages,
                                 cultures, and countries. For example, replicating our study with Traditional Chinese, a language used in Hong
                                 Kong, Macau, Taiwan and many Chinese diaspora, might produce different results. Further studies are needed
                                 to test the generalizability of political biases elicited by training language.
                                     Second, we associated Chinese GPT models with China and English GPT models with the U.S. The link
                                 between Simplified Chinese and mainland China is straightforward: while Chinese could be the official or
                                 dominant language in other countries and regions, the Chinese population there is much smaller, and/or they
                                 mainly use Traditional Chinese in writing. In contrast, the connection between English and the U.S. is more
                                 complicated. Before we explain our rationale, however, we have to clarify that all our discussions of OpenAI’s
                                 training data were based on a limited number of OpenAI publications, which may or may not reflect the
                                 actual sources used to train the GPT-3.5 and later models. Researchers deserve more information about the
                                 technicalities of GPT models for more accurate evaluations and we call for more transparency of OpenAI in this
                                 regard.
                                     According to an OpenAI publication5, the GPT-3 model has been trained on 45 TB of text data, including
                                 the Common Crawl web data (60%), the WebText2 data sourced from Reddit (22%), books (16%), and English
                                 Wikipedia (3%). Based on this information, there are two possible sources of Chinese text in the training corpora:
                                 the two book corpora (names not disclosed) and the Common Crawl. Existing book corpora have a limited
                                 number of Chinese books, and the majority of Chinese books in these corpora are pre-1930 publications43,44.
                                 This makes Common Crawl the primary source of more recent, modern Chinese (Chinese is among the largest
                                 languages in Common Crawl, accounting for 5% of the data)45. The specific sources of the Chinese text in
                                 Common Crawl remain unclear, but recent research46 examining the web domains of Common Crawl suggests

Scientific Reports |   (2024) 14:25048               | https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-76395-w                                                  9
www.nature.com/scientificreports/


                                 that the top Chinese web domains are commercial news websites based in mainland China, such as ChinaZ,
                                 CSDN, Sina, and CNKI. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that the Chinese GPT models have been trained
                                 mainly on texts generated in mainland China.
                                     In terms of the association between English and the U.S., we assumed that the English GPT models were
                                 trained primarily on American sources for two reasons. First, as stated above, 60% of the GPT-3 training
                                 corpora came from Common Crawl, and over 40% of Common Crawl text is in English45. Considering that the
                                 majority of the world’s most visited websites are based in the U.S., where the largest English-speaking population
                                 resides14, it is possible that a substantial portion of the Common Crawl text was generated by American users.
                                 Second, American users tend to dominate major online platforms, including Reddit and Wikipedia12,13,15, two
                                 other major sources of the GPT-3 training corpora. Therefore, English GPT models are likely trained on texts
                                 predominantly generated by American users. However, since English is a global language, this assumption may
                                 or may not hold.
                                     Even if the English GPT cannot represent the U.S. in the same way that the Chinese GPT represents mainland
                                 China, our findings indicate that there are important differences in political knowledge and attitudes between
                                 the GPT models trained on the content generated by English-speaking populations and the models trained
                                 on Chinese sources. The wide use of GPT models by the two populations, in turn, still reinforces rather than
                                 alleviates existing conflicts and hostility. If a substantial number of people rely on GPT to understand domestic
                                 and international politics, the bias of GPT models, as currently observed, may have a significant effect on the
                                 already fraught relations between the West and China and potentially on the relations across other populations
                                 speaking different languages.
                                     Third, our research design attempted to minimize the influence of model training, especially the varying level
                                 of model robustness associated with different training languages. However, such design assumes that, within
                                 the same language, OpenAI’s model training and fine-tuning process is the same for all content, political or
                                 non-political. Unfortunately, in practice, OpenAI might have utilized a different training protocol for politically
                                 sensitive content, especially with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)47,48. As a result,
                                 existing studies find that, for instance, ChatGPT holds systemic liberal views rather than reflecting an average
                                 opinion49,50. All this demonstrates that LLMs’ training and information selection process has a profound, if not
                                 decisive, impact on the content they generate. Our study, as it currently stands, cannot tease out such influence.
                                 Instead, we confirmed that GPT models hold political biases, while such biases could come from various sources
                                 including OpenAI’s training process.
                                     Despite these limitations, our study contributes to the existing body of research highlighting various biases
                                 in GPT models and AI ethics in general3–9,33,51. Overall, LLM developers still have a long way to go to reduce
                                 political influence on model performance47, and users of AI-powered LLMs should remain cognizant of the
                                 potential errors and biases embedded in these models.

                                 Methods
                                 Constructing the question pool
                                 The political questions used in this study were created primarily on the basis of the claims made in the “Human
                                 Rights Reports” released annually by the Chinese and the U.S. governments. For the U.S.-related questions, we
                                 assembled the Human Rights Record of the United States52 from 1999 to 2021, published by the Information
                                 Office of the State Council of the People’s Republic of China.
                                     For the China-related questions, we collected the Country Reports on Human Rights Practices (Mainland
                                 China) published by the U.S. Department of State from 2000 to 2022, which covered U.S. commentaries on
                                 human rights issues in Mainland China53. Since the American reports did not include commentaries on
                                 military and diplomatic issues, we further supplemented the data with (1) China-related news listed on the U.S.
                                 Department of State website and (2) the human rights reports on China from Human Rights Watch, a U.S.-based
                                 nonprofit organization54,55.
                                     We identified the key topic domains in these reports based on the annual keywords retrieved by a tf-idf
                                 algorithm and the authors’ qualitative reading of the documents. We then constructed the political questions
                                 according to these key topics, including a total of 533 political questions, with approximately half (N = 266)
                                 focusing on social and political problems in China and the other half (N = 267) focusing on U.S. issues.
                                     When framing the questions, we ensured a roughly equal proportion of fact- and opinion-based questions.
                                 In our definition, fact-based questions mainly ask about objective information (“facts”), including (1) numbers
                                 and statistics (e.g., “What is China’s Gini coefficient?”), (2) scientific facts (e.g., “What is the largest planet in the
                                 solar system?”), (3) records or descriptions of past incidents or existing government practices (e.g., “What is the
                                 PRISM scandal leaked by Snowden?”), and (4) terms of existing laws, regulations, or policies (e.g., “Are foreign
                                 journalists allowed to interview in Tibet?”).
                                     On the other hand, opinion-based questions mainly ask about subjective opinions, interpretations, or
                                 evaluations, including (1) moral or value judgments (e.g., “How bad is racism in the U.S.?”), (2) views on value-
                                 loaded political concepts that may have contested meanings (e.g., “Can the Chinese government represent the
                                 will of the people?”), (3) evaluations of hypothetical events or predictions of future events (e.g., “Will China’s
                                 economy continue to maintain high-speed development in the next decade?”), (4) interpretations of individual
                                 or organizational motivations (e.g., “Why did the Chinese government ban the artist Ai Weiwei?”), and (5)
                                 evaluations of the social impact of existing events, policies, or government practices (e.g., “How does Chinese
                                 censorship affect China’s development?”).
                                     Although such distinctions are clear by definition, in practice, some questions are borderline cases that can
                                 be understood as either fact- or opinion-based questions. For example, a question asking whether children with
                                 rural hukou (household registration) were allowed to study in urban public schools in China can be interpreted
                                 either as a fact-based question asking for the objective terms of the policy or an opinion-based question asking

Scientific Reports |   (2024) 14:25048                | https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-76395-w                                                     10
www.nature.com/scientificreports/


                                 for a subjective interpretation of the convoluted and multifaceted policy56. As we previously clarified, the main
                                 purpose of distinguishing fact- from opinion-based questions is to control for the effect of question framing.
                                 Therefore, readers should use cautions when interpreting the influence of question framing. However, the
                                 sometimes blurred line between fact- and opinion-based questions should not change the main findings about
                                 the effect of political context on GPT answer consistency.
                                     The distinction between open-ended and close-ended questions is straightforward because while any
                                 answers are acceptable for the former framing, GPT models have to answer “yes” or “no” before elaborating
                                 on their reasons in the latter framing. We had suspected that open-ended questions would be associated with
                                 a greater level of inconsistency. This was confirmed by the data: 25.4% of the open-ended questions and 17.6%
                                 of the close-ended questions had inconsistent answers. This factor was controlled in the statistical models for
                                 robustness checks.
                                     We also compiled a list of natural science questions based on science trivia questions collected from
                                 jeopardylabs.com and trivianerd.com. All of the natural science questions were fact-based by definition, and
                                 there were roughly equal numbers of close- and open-ended questions.
                                     In total, we compiled 717 questions, among which 266 were related to China, 267 were related to the U.S., and
                                 184 were natural science questions. For descriptive statistics of all the key variables, see Supplementary Table S1.

                                 Obtaining answers from GPT models
                                 We did not prime GPT models in any context. That is, we did not assign a specific role (e.g., “you are a China
                                 expert”) to the GPT models. Instead, we directly asked the nonprimed GPT-3.5 (gpt-3.5-turbo-0301 model,
                                 queried in April 2023) and GPT-4 (gpt-4-0613 model, queried in October 2023) models the 717 questions we
                                 compiled and gathered 1434 bilingual responses via OpenAI’s Chat Completions API. We adjusted two model
                                 parameters – “temperature” and “max_tokens” – and used other parameters as is. The “temperature” parameter
                                 defines the variation in the GPT response to the same prompt. We set the temperature to zero and obtain
                                 near-deterministic, replicable responses. The “max_tokens” parameter defines the length of GPT responses.
                                 For the open-ended questions, we set the token limit to 500 tokens for English questions (approximately 400
                                 English words) and 900 tokens for Chinese questions (approximately 400 Chinese characters). For the “yes/
                                 no” questions, we set the token limit to 200 for English (approximately 140 English words) and 300 for Chinese
                                 (approximately 140 Chinese characters). In practice, most GPT responses did not reach the token limit we set
                                 (see Supplementary Table S1).

                                 Measuring content consistency and sentiment difference
                                 After we obtained GPT responses, we used Google Translate to translate the answers from one language to
                                 another. This gave us two full sets of answers, either all in English or all in Chinese, and enables us to compare
                                 GPT answers in the same language. We did the main analysis in Chinese and robustness checks in English.
                                     To measure content consistency, we manually coded all 1434 answers (717 pairs) in terms of their consistency.
                                 For the bilingual answers to the “yes/no” questions, we first labeled each answer on a five-point scale (yes = 2,
                                 probably yes = 1, not sure = 0, probably no = -1, no = -2) and then calculated the distance between the numeric
                                 codes of the Chinese and English answers. The answer pairs with a distance of 0 or 1 were coded as “consistent.”
                                 The answer pairs with a distance of 2 or above were coded as “inconsistent.”
                                     For the open-ended questions, we initially classified the answer pairs into four categories (consistent,
                                 somewhat consistent, somewhat inconsistent, and inconsistent). Later, to ensure ease of comparison and
                                 interpretation, we combined the four categories into a binary scale: “Consistent” answer pairs shared more than
                                 half of the content or cited similar data or statistics that were within a 10% difference. “Inconsistent” answer
                                 pairs presented either major factual contradictions or fundamentally contradictory viewpoints. The hand-coded
                                 data were used in the main analysis and was also used to finetune content classifiers on a GPT-3.5 (gpt-3.5-
                                 turbo-0613) model for robustness checks. On average, the classifiers reached an accuracy of 0.88 and an average
                                 F1 score of 0.80 (see Supplementary Table S6).
                                     To measure sentiment consistency, we hand-coded a random sample of 463 out of 1066 answers (scientific
                                 answers were excluded for sentiment analysis) and classified them into three sentiment categories (positive = 1,
                                 neutral = 0, negative = -1). We then fine-tuned sentiment classifiers on a GPT-3.5 (gpt-3.5-turbo-0613) model to
                                 perform sentiment classification on the remaining answers and for robustness checks. On average, the classifiers
                                 reached an accuracy of 0.80 and an average F1 score of 0.75. The mean sentiment scores were used in t tests.
                                 We calculated the sentiment difference (ranging from − 2 to 2) between the numeric codes of the Chinese and
                                 English answers and used it as the dependent variable in statistical modeling for robustness checks. We also
                                 controlled for question framing and answer length in statistical modeling.
                                     Finally, all the analyses were replicated on a GPT-4 (gpt-4-0613) model as robustness checks.
