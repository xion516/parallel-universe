  ABSTRACT Search engines serve as primary gateways to information, and users tend to trust them,
  despite being opaque algorithms and customizing results in non-transparent ways. Personalization, such
  as tailoring content based on a user’s location, is useful for routine content searches (e.g., finding nearby
  restaurants). However, this same personalization becomes problematic – and potentially harmful – when
  users seek information on sensitive or contentious geopolitical topics. In this work, we present the first
  large-scale measurement of how search engine curation and personalization affect users across different
  countries with distinct regional, political, and cultural traits. We developed a novel, privacy-preserving, bot-
  based methodology that simulates online user profiles embedded with cumulative regional traits. These
  customized bots were deployed from different locations and conducted identical searches, either on the
  Israel-Palestine conflict (e.g. ‘‘Hamas’’) or neutral queries (e.g. ‘‘dinner recipe’’), using both Google and
  DuckDuckGo. Our study addresses two main questions: 1) How do search results for the same query
  differ across countries, and which factors drive these differences?; and 2) Does personalization vary more
  significantly for conflict-related or for general queries? Our findings show that search result rankings are
  significantly influenced by location, interface language, and simulated browsing context, with the strongest
  effects observed for conflict-related queries. In many cases, these differences led to non-neutral portrayals
  of the same event, depending on the user’s presumed region. These results highlight how search engine
  personalization can shape public understanding of global events, creating fragmented or biased informational
  environments. This underscores the importance of educating users about the invisible filters that influence
  their access to information and, from a policy standpoint, these findings reveal a pressing need for greater
  transparency and accountability in how search engines personalize politically sensitive content. This is
  especially urgent given their outsized role in shaping democratic discourse and public opinion worldwide.
