                                                    Large Language Models increasingly serve as interfaces for knowledge-intensive
                                                    information seeking tasks across languages by synthesizing multilingual evidence.
                                                    Prior work has shown that they often exhibit query-language preference—the ten-
                                                    dency to favor sources written in the language of the query—but has largely ex-

                                                    languages. However, this bias becomes consequential when sources in different
                                                    languages provide incomplete or inconsistent accounts of the same fact, since the
                                                    information users receive then depends on the sources a model selects to use. To

                                                    parities, we introduce WALDO,1 a multilingual Question-Answering (QA) bench-

                                         1       I NTRODUCTION
                                         Large Language Models (LLMs) increasingly mediate knowledge-intensive information seeking
                                         tasks across diverse languages (Longpre et al., 2021a). When answering a user’s query, they may
                                         draw on information from sources in multiple languages and select which sources inform their final
                                         responses (Wu et al., 2024; Chirkova et al., 2024). As LLMs become interfaces between users and
                                         multilingual information ecosystems (Nadalic Sotic & Kamps, 2026), understanding how they select
                                         among competing knowledge sources becomes increasingly important.
                                         A growing body of evidence suggests that multilingual systems often exhibit query-language prefer-
                                         ence during generation: a tendency to favor knowledge sources written in the same language as the
                                         user query (Ki et al., 2026). Consequently, the language in which a user poses a question can influ-
                                         ence which sources inform the response, and therefore what information the user ultimately receives.
                                         This is not necessarily problematic when equivalent information is available across languages—an
                                         assumption underlying much prior work (Park & Lee, 2025; Qi et al., 2025). In practice, however,
                                         multilingual sources are rarely perfectly redundant or mutually consistent (Filatova, 2009).

Query-language preference becomes consequential when knowledge is heterogeneous across lan-
guages, which can take two forms. First, knowledge may be unevenly available: a fact documented
in one language may be absent from sources in another, creating a knowledge gap (Hecht & Gergle,
2010). Second, sources in different languages may provide incompatible accounts of the same fact,
resulting in a knowledge conflict (Li et al., 2024a; Cattan et al., 2025). When neither account can
be resolved using available signals, semantically equivalent queries in different languages should
surface the gap or conflict rather than elicit different responses depending on the query language. At
scale, these differences can create asymmetries in answer quality (Jin et al., 2024a), produce uneven
downstream performance (Ranaldi et al., 2026), deepen opinion polarization (Sharma et al., 2024),
reinforce the marginalization of underrepresented narratives (Phillipson, 1992), and ultimately un-
dermine user trust in multilingual information systems (Arguedas et al., 2023). These risks motivate
the study of query-language preference under cross-lingual knowledge disparities.
To this end, we introduce WALDO, a multilingual Question-Answering (QA) benchmark for evalu-
ating query-language preference under knowledge gaps and knowledge conflicts (§3). As illustrated
in Figure 1, WALDO constructs QA instances around target facts that satisfy two criteria: they ap-
pear in Wikipedia documents in languages other than English but are absent from the corresponding
English documents (§3.1), and they postdate the reported knowledge cutoffs of the evaluated model.
This design helps minimize the possibility that models answer from their parametric knowledge,
enabling observed response differences to be more directly attributed to their use of the context
(Jin et al., 2024b). We additionally introduce factually inconsistent versions of the target facts by
manually perturbing the corresponding English Wikipedia documents to simulate real-world knowl-
edge conflict scenarios. By holding the available multilingual context fixed while varying only the
query language, WALDO enables a counterfactual test of whether query language alone changes the
knowledge source on which a model bases its answer.
Using WALDO, we evaluate eight models across five languages (§4) and diagnose the extent to which
they exhibit query-language preference. Under knowledge gaps, models largely overcome this pref-
erence, producing consistent responses across query languages. Under knowledge conflicts, how-
ever, their response distributions differ substantially by query language (§5). These findings motivate
our exploration of potential approaches for mitigating query-language preference in the presence of
conflicting evidence. We introduce Query-language Preference Head Ablation (QPHA) and adapt
Quantized Low-Rank Adapter (QL O RA) training (Dettmers et al., 2023) as two targeted meth-

2      BACKGROUND : D EFINITION , E VALUATION , AND M ITIGATION
Definition. Language preference refers to a model’s systematic tendency to favor knowledge sources
in some languages over others. Such preference may arise from unequal language representation
in pre-training data (Joshi et al., 2020) or from cross-lingual tokenization differences (Petrov et al.,
2023). Query-language preference is a specific form of language preference in which a model favors
sources written in the same language as the query (Ki et al., 2026). Prior work has studied how
query-language preference manifests in parametric knowledge (Goldman et al., 2025), multilingual
retrieval (Yang et al., 2024; Park & Lee, 2025), and generation (Sharma et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a;
Ki et al., 2026). We focus on generation, holding the multilingual retrieved context fixed to isolate
how query language affects the knowledge source on which a model bases its response.
Datasets and Evaluation. Existing work studies query-language preference in two broad settings:
settings that assume semantically equivalent knowledge is available across languages, and simu-
lated settings with limited ecological validity (de Vries et al., 2020). One line of work retrieves
naturally occurring evidence from real-world sources (Qi et al., 2025; Park et al., 2026; Huo et al.,
2026; Wang et al., 2026). However, they cannot isolate query-language preference when language
editions differ in the availability or content of relevant facts. Conversely, Sharma et al. (2025) in-
troduces cross-lingual knowledge conflicts, but uses fictional documents. A separate body of work
documents cross-lingual knowledge gaps (Massa & Scrinzi, 2012; Bao et al., 2012; Miquel-Ribé &
Laniado, 2018; 2020; Hupkes & Bogoychev, 2025) as well as factual and narrative inconsistencies
across Wikipedia language editions (Samir et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024a; Zhao et al., 2026a). Yet,
this work generally neither provides a reusable benchmark nor connects such disparities to query-
language preference. WALDO addresses these gaps by targeting naturally occurring, cross-lingually
heterogeneous facts from Wikipedia, covering knowledge gaps and controlled knowledge conflicts.
Mitigation. Prior efforts to mitigate language preference have primarily targeted the knowledge-
source retrieval stage, including calibrated relevance estimation (Park et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026;
Jang et al., 2026), query translation (Amiraz et al., 2025), and language-controlled retrieval (Huo
et al., 2026; Qi et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2026; Hong et al., 2026). At the generation stage, existing ap-
proaches translate retrieved documents into English (Ranaldi et al., 2026) or into the query language
(Park & Lee, 2025) to unify the evidence in a single pivot language. To our knowledge, no prior
work has specifically studied query-language preference at generation time or explored mechanistic
interventions or fine-tuning approaches to mitigate it; we take a first step toward doing so.

3      C REATING WALDO
Wikipedia is a valuable source of cross-lingual knowledge disparities: documents about the same
entity in different language editions are not merely translations of one another (Hecht, 2013), but
contain localized knowledge (Haraway, 1988; Kumar, 2021) shaped by the distinct contributor com-
munities that produce and maintain them (Hale, 2014; Sen et al., 2015; Johnson & Lescak, 2022).

For each source language, we collect Wikipedia documents and their contributor-authored revision
histories from publicly available Wikipedia dumps available as of June 1, 2026.3 We retain revisions
made on or after January 1, 2020, representing each revision as a diff to its parent revision.
Source Languages. We collect documents in five source languages spanning diverse resource levels,
language families, scripts, and Wikipedia sizes: French (fr), Simplified Chinese (zh), Japanese (ja),
Korean (ko), and Bengali (bn). Per-language characteristics are detailed in Appendix A.1.
We refer to the complete collection of revisions per language as the full set, which we plan to release.
For our experiments, we sample five dump files per language to form a balanced evaluation set.

3.1        K NOWLEDGE G AP [    ]

Heuristics & LLM Annotation. To retain meaningful revisions for evaluation, we filter candidate
revisions using a combination of heuristic rules and LLM-based annotation with GPT-5.6-T ERRA
(OpenAI, 2026b). After manual auditing, we define a suite of rules that a revision must (1) not
be a bot edit or maintenance-related change (e.g., a revert); (2) have a length within a predefined
range; (3) introduce at least one factual statement without removing information; and (4) contain an
introduced fact that remains in the latest version of the source-language Wikipedia document but is
absent from the latest linked English document, identified via the same Wikidata QID.
During LLM annotation, we also record a timestamp, formatted as YYYY-MM, indicating the
month in which the introduced fact is asserted to have occurred. This procedure retains, on average,
0.3% of candidate revisions across languages. See Appendix A.2 for filtering details, examples of
excluded revisions, and per-language retain rates.
QA Generation. For each retained revision, we provide GPT-5.6-T ERRA with the revision, its
source-language document, and the corresponding English document. The model generates aligned
QA pairs in the source language (Qsrc , Asrc ) and English (Qen , Aen ), which are translations of one
another and target the same fact introduced by the revision. We retain a revision only if the source-
language and English sets contain the same number of QA pairs and every aligned QA pair achieves
an X COMET translation-quality score of at least 80.0 (Guerreiro et al., 2023). Further details and
per-language X COMET scores are provided in Appendix A.3.

3.2        K NOWLEDGE C ONFLICT [     ]

Conflict Injection & QA Generation. For each revision retained in §3.1, we introduce a controlled
knowledge conflict by prompting an LLM (GPT-5.6-T ERRA) to inject an inconsistent version of the
target fact into the corresponding English document and update the answers to the same questions
(Chen et al., 2022; Mo et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026a;b). To simulate real-world knowledge con-
flicts, we follow prior conflict-injection practices (Longpre et al., 2021b; Wang et al., 2024). Each
injected conflict must satisfy two criteria: (1) it modifies a core attribute of the target fact, such as a
name, quantity, date, or location, rather than merely negating it (e.g., “is” → “is not”); and (2) it is
applied consistently throughout the document while preserving the original tone. Table 1 reports the
distribution of conflict types per language, with numerical and date changes being the most common
on average. Appendix A.4 provides qualitative examples of each conflict type.
Answerability Check. To verify that each conflict-injected English document supports its revised
answer, we use GPT-5.6-S OL, a different model from the generator, to answer each original English
question using the conflict-injected document. We retain only QA pairs that pass this answerability
check (Sun et al., 2026); on average, 98.8% of pairs are retained across languages. Our final evalua-
tion set contains approximately 12K QA pairs across five source languages, each of which passes the
procedures for both knowledge gaps (§3.1) and conflicts (§3.2). More details are in Appendix A.5.
For each evaluated model, we retain only QA pairs whose (1) timestamp postdates the model’s
reported knowledge cutoff and (2) when given only the question, the model answers incorrectly in
both the source language and English, giving an average retention rate of 23.8%. This reduces the
likelihood that a model can answer from parametric knowledge alone (Yeh & Li, 2026) or preferen-
tially follows evidence consistent with its prior beliefs (i.e., confirmation bias; Jin et al. (2024a)). It
therefore allows observed response differences to be attributed more directly to the provided context.

5                     D IAGNOSIS R ESULTS
Figure 2 presents our diagnosis for query-language preference.5 We highlight four findings below.
Closed-source models largely overcome query-language preference under knowledge gaps. As
shown in Figure 2 (top), closed-source models show little to no statistically significant difference
in answer agreement across query languages. In contrast, open-weight models exhibit significant
agreement gaps: 15.9% for LL A MA-3.1, 12.1% for Q WEN -3, and 18.2% for AYA -E XPANSE. No-
tably, among the three, AYA -E XPANSE shows the largest gap despite explicit training on diverse
languages and multilingual tasks (Dang et al., 2024). Answer probability shows a similar pattern:
all three open-weight models assign significantly different likelihoods to the source-supported an-
swer across query languages. These findings suggest that, when the required knowledge to correctly
answer a query is available only in one language, closed-source models generally recover it regard-
less of the query language, whereas the smaller open-weight models often fail to.

6     M ITIGATION FOR K NOWLEDGE C ONFLICTS
Under conflicting evidence across languages, models strongly favor the document in the query lan-
guage (§5). As an initial exploration of how this behavior may be mitigated, we study two targeted
approaches (§6.1) and evaluate their effectiveness in reducing query-language preference (§6.2).

the desired and conflicting answers across documents to define its objectives. Given these trade-offs,
future work could develop more robust approaches that retain the advantages of both methods.

C ONCLUSION & D ISCUSSION
We study query-language preference in multilingual systems under two forms of cross-lingual
knowledge disparities. Using WALDO, we find that models largely overcome this preference under
knowledge gaps, but exhibit it strongly under conflicts, which can be effectively mitigated through
a mechanistic intervention or L O RA training. More broadly, addressing query-language preference
is important for equitable multilingual information access. If the information a system surfaces
depends on a user’s query language, it may confine users to language-bounded evidence, reinforc-
ing filter bubbles (Flaxman et al., 2016) and information cocoons (Sunstein, 2018). An important
next step is to evaluate the real-world impact in deployment, including how source selection affects
users’ comprehension, trust, and information access. Future benchmarks should also extend beyond
Wikipedia to multilingual sources with different authority structures and update dynamics, such
as academic papers. We hope that WALDO supports research on robust cross-lingual knowledge
transfer and that our mitigation approaches serve as useful tools for studying and mitigating source-
selection biases beyond query language, including biases related to formality (Li et al., 2024b),
relevance (Wan et al., 2024), recency (Fang et al., 2025), and source origin (Schuster et al., 2026).

E THICS STATEMENT

Research on query-language preference in multilingual systems raises fairness concerns, particu-
larly for speakers of low-resource and underrepresented languages. When models provide different
answers to semantically equivalent queries solely because they are expressed in different languages,
they may reinforce existing disparities in access to reliable information. Our work aims to identify,
measure, and mitigate such disparities rather than reinforce them. Nevertheless, the proposed mit-
igation methods should be evaluated carefully across languages and domains before deployment,
as reducing one form of language preference may have uneven effects on answer quality, source
faithfulness, or linguistic and cultural coverage.

A.2      H EURISTICS & LLM A NNOTATION

Heuristics. We conduct a manual audit of a sample of sourced Wikipedia revisions to develop a
filtering procedure for our controlled evaluation. Based on this audit, we define a suite of heuristic
rules that retain only revisions meeting our criteria. We apply the following rules sequentially:

 • Required added text: Drop a row when the added text content is missing, non-string, or empty
   after trimming whitespace.
 • Remove bot edits: Drop a row if either the edit comment or contributor username contains a
   language-specific bot term.
 • Remove maintenance edits: Drop a row if its comment matches language-specific mainte-
   nance keywords such as typo/spelling fixes, links, references, formatting, categories, templates,
   cleanup, or wikification.
 • Remove reverts: Drop a row if its comment contains language-specific revert indicators, in-
   cluding terms like revert, undo, rv, vandal, or their equivalents in other source languages.
 • Remove high-symbol, low-content edits: Drop a row if either comment or the added text has
   at least 12 characters, at least 60% non-content symbols, and no more than 8 alphanumeric or
   source-script characters.
 • Remove markup-heavy additions: Drop a row if the added text contains wiki or HTML
   markup patterns and either at least half of its characters are non-content symbols or it contains
   four or more pipe characters (|).
 • Remove repetition: Drop a row if the added text contains a character repeated at least 8 con-
   secutive times.
 • Require punctuation: Drop a row if the added text does not contain a language-specific sen-
   tence terminator.
 • Require source-language text: Use LINGUA language detector tool8 on the added text and drop
   a row if it is undetectable or detected as a language other than the current source language.
 • Remove length outliers: Compute character-length quartiles of non-empty added text content,
   then drop rows outside Tukey’s Q1 − 1.5 × IQR to Q3 + 1.5 × IQR bounds (Tukey, 1977).

a binary verdict and rationale for whether: (1) the added text introduces at least one factual statement
without removing existing information, and (2) the added text is absent from the latest version of the
corresponding English Wikipedia document, identified using the same Wikidata QID. To determine

 • Adding the first paragraph. We add the first paragraph of each Wikipedia article as additional
   context, because it often provides a high-level overview of the topic. This variant increases
   the retention rate from 8% to 9%, corresponding to one additional retained revision. However,
   manual inspection found that this additional example did not satisfy our filtering criteria. We
   therefore decide not to include the first paragraph in the final prompt.
 • Providing Wikipedia context. We add a brief definition of Wikipedia to provide additional
   context, which led to no observable change in the retention rate (8%), so we omit this definition.
 • Removing the constraint on deleted text. We test removing the instruction that the deleted text
   must not remove meaningful information, to accommodate revisions that replace an incorrect
   fact with contradictory factual information. However, without this constraint, the LLM fails to
   retain some revisions that simultaneously add a standalone fact and remove unrelated content.
   We therefore retain the constraint in the final prompt.
 • Allowing general knowledge. We instruct the LLM to use both the provided context and general
   commonsense knowledge during the annotation. In the manual audit, this helped filter revisions
   containing irrelevant jokes or degenerate edits, thus we retain this instruction.
 • Translating the prompt. We translate the full prompt into the source language for revisions in
   that language. This variant did not yield an observable overall improvement and missed several
   revisions judged meaningful in the manual audit. We use the English prompt for all languages.

I NFO GAP Framework. As an alternative to LLM annotation for determining whether added text
appears in the latest English Wikipedia document, we consider I NFO G AP (Samir et al., 2024). I N -
FO G AP identifies information overlaps and gaps between documents in different languages through
three stages: (1) fact decomposition, which extracts atomic facts from each document; (2) multilin-
gual alignment, which aligns facts across language versions; and (3) verification, which determines
whether aligned facts are semantically equivalent.
Given an English document, we decompose its content into a set of atomic facts using the fact-
decomposition prompt of Samir et al. (2024) and the same model used for LLM annotation, GPT-
5.6-T ERRA. For multilingual alignment, we embed the decomposed English facts and the added
text using L A BSE (Feng et al., 2022). For each English fact, we compute a hubness score, which
is the average cosine similarity to its top-k nearest neighbors, and select the highest-scoring facts
as alignment candidates. We then use GPT-5.6-T ERRA to verify each candidate. If at least one
candidate fact survives verification, we label the added text as “match”; otherwise, as “no match.”
Compared with our LLM annotation approach, I NFO G AP produces a slightly lower retention rate.
This difference primarily arises when the English document contains only part of the information in

B       A DDITIONAL D IAGNOSIS WITH WALDO
B.1      R ETRIEVAL -AUGMENTED ROBUSTNESS R ESULTS

Our primary experiments provide paired multilingual Wikipedia documents directly to models in
order to isolate the effect of query language on evidence use. To test whether the pattern persists with
additional retrieved evidence, we evaluate 100 randomly sampled QA pairs in a retrieval-augmented
setting. For each question, we use OpenAI’s web-search tool to retrieve up to five documents from
external web sources.9 We then sample two retrieved documents in the source language and two in
English, and provide these four documents together with the original source-language and English
Wikipedia documents. Each model therefore receives six documents in total.
As shown in Table 10, adding retrieved documents generally lowers absolute answer agreement
and probability for both source-language and English queries. Manual inspection suggests that
it introduces additional candidate sources, which increases the difficulty of identifying the target
fact. Despite this overall reduction, we observe the same pattern as in the controlled two-document
setting: answer agreement and probability gaps are small under knowledge gaps but substantially
larger under knowledge conflicts. These results suggest that query-language preference is not solely
an artifact of the controlled two-document context.

B.2      P ER - LANGUAGE R ESULTS

We report the per-language results for each knowledge gap and conflict setting in Tables 21 and 22.
Across all five source languages, we observe that the general trend holds: query-language prefer-
ence is minimal under knowledge gaps but pronounced under conflicts. While answer agreement
shows no clear pattern by language resource level (Table 9), answer probability gap is consistently
larger for lower-resource languages: under knowledge gaps, 18.3% (Bengali), 15.2% (Korean),
14.1% (Japanese), 10.7% (Chinese), and 8.4% (French); under conflicts, 28.0% (Bengali), 25.8%
(Japanese), 23.5% (Korean), 17.8% (Chinese), and 12.6% (French).

B.5   K NOWLEDGE PARITY

To verify that the observed query-language preference is significantly reduced when equivalent
knowledge is available across languages, we introduce a control condition with cross-lingual knowl-
edge parity, which corresponds to another cell in Table 11. In this condition, the source-language
and English documents support the same target fact and answer: we inject an English translation
of the target fact into the English document. As shown in Table 12, the query-language preference
gaps measured by answer agreement and answer probability are generally close to zero.


C     H YPOTHESES

To identify potential causes of query-language preference and rule out plausible confounders, we
formulate and test four hypotheses. We introduce each hypothesis below.

C.1   P RE - TRAINING E FFECT

    For a fixed answer, models assign higher conditional probability when the query and docu-
    ment are in the same language than when they are in different languages.

Rationale. Multilingual language models are pre-trained primarily on large, language-specific web
corpora, whereas explicitly aligned cross-lingual text, such as parallel or bilingual documents, is
comparatively limited (Burchell et al., 2025). Consequently, semantically aligned same-language
query–document pairs may be more common in pre-training data than equivalent cross-language
pairs, potentially inducing a higher prior for same-language query–document combinations.
Test. Using three open-weight models, we compare the perplexity of a fixed candidate answer when
the query language either matches or differs from the language of the supporting document. For
each supported answer Aℓ and its document Dℓ , where ℓ ∈ {src, en}, we compute:

                          ∆ℓPPL = PPL(Aℓ | Q¬ℓ , Dℓ ) − PPL(Aℓ | Qℓ , Dℓ ) ,                                       (11)
where ¬ℓ denotes the other query language. For ℓ = en, Den and Aen refer to the conflict-injected
English document and its supported answer, respectively. A positive gap indicates that the fixed an-
swer has higher perplexity, and therefore lower likelihood, when the query and document languages
differ. A gap near zero indicates insensitivity to query–document language alignment.

C.2   E MBEDDING S IMILARITY


   Models are more likely to attribute an answer to the document matching the query language
   because same-language query–document pairs have higher embedding similarity than cross-
   language pairs.

Rationale. Retrieval-augmented models may favor documents that are more similar to the query in
embedding space. If multilingual representations systematically assign higher similarity to same-
language than cross-language query–document pairs, this bias could lead models to preferentially
cite the document written in the query language.
Test. For each of the three open-weight models, we compute cosine similarity between each query
and the source-language and conflict-injected English documents using the model’s own embed-
dings. For a query in language ℓ ∈ {src, en}, let Dℓ denote the document in the query language and
D¬ℓ the document in the other language. We compute the same-language similarity advantage:

            ∆ℓSim = cos(embed(Qℓ ), embed(Dℓ )) − cos(embed(Qℓ ), embed(D¬ℓ )) .                (12)

For English queries, Den denotes the conflict-injected English document. A positive value indicates
that the query is more similar to the same-language document, while a value near zero indicates little
sensitivity to query–document language alignment.
We then compute the point-biserial correlation between ∆ℓSim and citation attribution, a binary indi-
cator of whether the model cites the document in the query language (Appendix B.4). A significant
positive correlation indicates that models are more likely to cite the same-language document when
it has a larger embedding-similarity advantage, supporting the hypothesis.
Result. As shown in Table 15, correlations between ∆ℓSim and citation attribution are mixed across
models and languages, with only a small number of model–language combinations showing a sta-
tistically significant positive correlation. Thus, greater query–document embedding similarity does
not reliably predict query-language preference from an attribution perspective.

C.3   S URFACE C UES

   Query-language preference is driven by surface-level document properties, including relative
   document length and input order.

Rationale. Language models can be sensitive to superficial properties of evidence that affect their
predictions (Wan et al., 2024). Thus, observed query-language preference may instead reflect dif-
ferences in the source-language and English Wikipedia documents, rather than a preference for the
query language itself. We examine two potential confounds: relative document length and document
order in the input context. Although we randomize document order, residual position effects could
still influence which document a model follows (Liu et al., 2024).
Test. Using the three open-weight models, we measure document length in tokens using each
model’s tokenizer and compute the length difference:
                                                               ′
                             ∆length = Length(Dsrc ) − Length(Den ).                             (13)
Positive values indicate that the source-language document is longer. We also record whether the
source-language document appears first in the input context. For each surface cue, we compute its
Pearson correlation with the instance-level answer agreement difference between source-language
and English queries. A significant correlation would indicate that the cue is associated with observed
query-language preference.
Result. Table 16 shows that correlations between relative document length and the answer agree-
ment difference are generally negative across models and languages: as the source-language doc-
ument becomes longer relative to the English document, answer agreement gap tends to decrease.
However, most correlations are not statistically significant, providing little evidence that document
length reliably drives query-language preference.

C.4   T OPIC L OCALITY




   Query-language preference may reflect a query’s cultural specificity rather than the language
   in which it is asked.

Rationale. Native-language Wikipedia editions often cover regionally relevant topics that are ab-
sent from their English counterparts, a pattern associated with self-focus bias (Hecht & Gergle,
2009; Callahan & Herring, 2011) and situated knowledge (Haraway, 1988). Consequently, cultur-
ally specific queries may be better supported by documents in the associated language. Models might
therefore follow the source-language document because of topic locality rather than query language
itself. We test this possibility by comparing query-language preference for culturally specific and
culturally agnostic queries.
Test. Following Park et al. (2026), we use GPT-5.6-T ERRA to classify each QA pair as culturally
specific or agnostic using Prompt A.10 in Appendix E. Specific queries concern topics culturally
associated with the source language or its related region; agnostic queries do not require language-
or culture-specific knowledge.
We compare the instance-level answer agreement difference between source-language and English
queries across these two groups using Welch’s two-sample t-test. A statistically significant group
difference would indicate that topic locality contributes to observed query-language preference.
Result. As shown in Table 17, differences in the answer-agreement difference between culturally
specific and agnostic queries are not statistically significant. Thus, this analysis provides little evi-
dence that topic locality confounds query-language preference.

 Instruction: Your task is to swap the factual information between two Wikipedia passages while keeping
 each replacement in the language of its destination article.

 Produce exactly two strings:
 1. “swapped text”: express the complete meaning of conflict fact in the language of the source article. It
 will replace the source fact exactly.
 2. “swapped conflict sent”: express the complete meaning of source fact in the language of conflict
 article. It will replace conflict fact exactly.

 Rules:
 - Preserve the facts exactly when translating: names, dates, numbers, events, relations, and polarity must
 not change.
 - Match the destination’s language, grammar, capitalization, punctuation, and encyclopedic style.
 - A target may be a sentence fragment. Use its surrounding context to make the replacement fit the same
 grammatical position; do not force it into a standalone sentence.
 - Do not combine the old and new facts, explain the edit, or add citations.
 - Return only one JSON object with exactly the keys “swapped text” and “swapped conflict sent”.

 Source fact: {source fact}
 Source article : {source article}
 Conflict fact: {conflict fact}
 Conflict article : {conflict article}

 Prompt A.10. Topic Locality Classification
 Instruction: You are given a list of English questions (English query) with their English answers
 (English answer), and the corresponding original {lang} questions (Source-language query) and
 answers (Source-language answer) they were translated from. The i-th items across all four lists express
 the same question/answer pair.

 Your task is to classify each question/answer pair as exactly one of:
 - “specific”: the fact is tied to {lang} language, culture, geography, history, institutions, or audience;
 the kind of fact that is unlikely to be covered by a source that isn’t specifically about that context. -
 “agnostic”: the fact is general knowledge that isn’t inherently tied to that {lang} context, even though
 it happens to appear in this {lang} Wikipedia article (e.g., a fact about a person, place, or event outside
 that context, or a universal/technical fact).

 Requirements:
 1. Produce exactly one label per question/answer pair, aligned by index.
 2. The label must be exactly “specific” or “agnostic” (lowercase, no other text).
 3. Ensure the output list has the same length as English query.

 Return only this JSON object with no other text:
 {{
 “classification”: [List of “specific” or “agnostic” labels, aligned by index with the English query]
 }}

 English query: {en query}
 English answer: {en answer}
 Source-language query: {src query}
 Source-language answer: {src answer}
